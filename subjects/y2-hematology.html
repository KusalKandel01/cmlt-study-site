06 Haematopoiesis (6 marks · 10 hrs)

Haematopoiesis is the ongoing formation of blood cells — in adults this happens almost entirely in the bone marrow, replacing the roughly one million red cells your body loses every second. All three cell lines below start from the same origin (a haematopoietic stem cell) and then diverge into separate development pathways.

  • Erythropoiesis (RBC formation) — progresses through defined stages as the developing cell shrinks, loses its nucleus, and fills with hemoglobin; regulated mainly by erythropoietin, a hormone released by the kidneys when they sense low oxygen — which is exactly why chronic kidney disease so often causes anemia. A mature RBC survives about 120 days before being removed by the spleen (its "fate").
  • Leucopoiesis (WBC formation) splits into three parallel series — myeloid (neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils), lymphocytic (lymphocytes), and monocytic (monocytes) — each regulated by its own set of growth factors, which is why a differential count (Unit 12) can reveal exactly which line is overproducing
  • Thrombopoiesis (platelet formation) — platelets are actually fragments budded off a single giant precursor cell (megakaryocyte), regulated primarily by the hormone thrombopoietin

07 Hemoglobin (5 marks · 9 hrs)

Hemoglobin is the iron-containing protein inside red cells that actually carries oxygen — its structure (four protein chains, each holding one iron-containing heme group) is exactly why any inherited change to those chains has such wide-reaching effects.

ConditionKey facts
HaemoglobinopathiesThe umbrella term for any inherited disorder of hemoglobin structure or synthesis — both conditions below are specific examples
Sickle cell anemiaA single amino-acid substitution distorts hemoglobin's shape, which then bends red cells into a rigid sickle shape under low oxygen — diagnosed by a sickling test or hemoglobin electrophoresis
ThalassemiaReduced production (not abnormal structure) of one hemoglobin chain, unbalancing the whole molecule — several types depending on which chain is affected, also confirmed by electrophoresis

08 Red Cell Disorders (8 marks · 13 hrs)

Red cell disorders are qualitative (wrong shape/content), quantitative (wrong number), or both. Anaemia — a reduced ability of blood to carry oxygen, whether from too few red cells or too little hemoglobin in each one — is classified by its underlying cause, which is exactly the logic behind the three named types below:

  • Iron deficiency anemia — the most common type worldwide; without enough iron, the bone marrow simply can't build functional hemoglobin, producing small, pale red cells
  • Megaloblastic anemia — caused by deficient vitamin B12 or folate, both needed for DNA synthesis during cell division; cells grow larger than normal because they can't divide properly
  • Hemolytic anemia — red cells are destroyed faster than the marrow can replace them, whether from an inherited defect (like sickle cell disease above) or an external cause (autoimmune, infection)

Polycythemia is the mirror-image disorder — an excess of red cell mass, thickening the blood and raising clotting risk, whether from bone marrow overproduction or as the body's response to chronic low oxygen.

09 White Blood Cell Disorders (5 marks · 8 hrs)

White cell disorders can be qualitative (abnormal function) or quantitative (too many/too few) — the two named conditions below sit at opposite ends of a spectrum that looks deceptively similar on a basic count:

TermDefinition
Leukemoid reactionA benign, reactive over-production of WBCs — the body's normal, appropriate response to something like a severe infection
LeukemiaA malignant WBC disorder — uncontrolled, abnormal proliferation of white cells that crowds out normal marrow function

A very common Group B question: differentiate between leukemoid reaction and leukemia — the key distinguishing clue is that a leukemoid reaction resolves once the underlying infection clears, while leukemia doesn't; blood film morphology and specific marker tests separate the two definitively.

12 Hematological Techniques (31 marks · 55 hrs)

The single highest-marks unit in this subject — nearly 40% of the total. This is the practical core of hematology: everything you learned about cell formation and disorders above becomes something you can actually measure and report here.

Sample collection

Anticoagulants stop a blood sample from clotting before it can be tested, and different ones work by different mechanisms (chelating calcium vs directly inhibiting clotting enzymes) — which is exactly why using the wrong anticoagulant tube for a given test gives a wrong result, not just a technical inconvenience.

  • Phlebotomy — capillary collection (a finger/heel prick) suits small-volume tests, venous collection is the standard for most lab work, and arterial collection is reserved for blood gas analysis specifically; each has its own anatomical sites and precautions to avoid hemolysis or contamination
  • Blood smear preparation — a thin smear spreads cells in a single layer for morphology and differential counting; a thick smear concentrates cells for detecting low-density parasites (directly reused in Unit 9's blood parasite detection)

Hemocytometry

Before automated cell counters existed, every count below was done manually with a hemocytometer (a precisely-ruled counting chamber) — know the principle, procedure, normal values, and clinical significance of the total RBC count, total WBC count, and platelet count, since these are the foundation every other calculated value in this unit builds on.

Staining & counts

  • Romanowsky stains (Wright's, Leishman's, Giemsa) all use a mix of acidic and basic dyes so different cell components pick up different colors — this is what makes cell types distinguishable under the microscope at all
  • Differential Leukocyte Count (DLC) — counts the proportion of each white cell type on a stained smear; an abnormal proportion (not just an abnormal total) is often the actual diagnostic clue, e.g. in the leukemoid-vs-leukemia distinction from Unit 5
  • Absolute Eosinophil Count — specifically useful for allergic conditions and parasitic infections, both of which drive eosinophils up
  • Bone marrow examination — when peripheral blood counts alone can't explain a disorder, sampling the marrow directly shows what's happening at the source

Named tests

TestKey facts
ESR (Wintrobe's & Westergren methods)Measures how fast red cells settle in a column over one hour — a non-specific marker that rises with inflammation of almost any cause
Packed Cell Volume (PCV)The percentage of blood volume occupied by red cells after centrifugation — directly reflects anemia or polycythemia
Hemoglobin estimationColorimetric (cyanmethaemoglobin, the reference method) and acid haematin (Sahli's, a simpler field method) both estimate hemoglobin concentration
Red cell indicesMCV, MCH, MCHC are all calculated (not directly measured) from the RBC count, hemoglobin, and PCV — they classify anemia by red cell size and hemoglobin content, directly distinguishing iron deficiency from megaloblastic anemia on paper alone
Reticulocyte countCounts young, still-maturing red cells — tells you whether the marrow is actively responding to anemia (high) or failing to respond (low)
Bleeding time / Clotting time / PT / APTTEach tests a different part of haemostasis (Unit 7) — screening for platelet-related vs coagulation-factor-related bleeding problems

Also know: the automated blood cell analyzer's working principle (electronic/optical counting instead of manual hemocytometry), LE cell preparation (an older autoimmune test), the osmotic fragility test and sickling test (both directly diagnostic for the red cell disorders in Unit 4), hemoglobin electrophoresis (confirms which hemoglobinopathy from Unit 3), and quality control in the hematology lab.

14 Immunohematology and Blood Banking (15 marks · 25 hrs)

Blood banking exists to solve one problem safely: moving blood from a healthy donor into a patient who needs it, without triggering a fatal immune reaction. Every procedure in this unit is built around preventing that single failure mode.

  • Blood grouping systems classify blood by which antigens sit on the red cell surface — mismatch a patient with the wrong group and their own antibodies attack the transfused cells
  • Donor selection/rejection criteria screen out donors whose blood could harm the recipient or whose own health could be put at risk by donating
  • Blood donation follows a strict prep → collection → transport → processing → storage chain, since blood components degrade differently and each has its own storage requirement
  • Blood components — whole blood is rarely transfused today; it's separated into red cells, plasma, and platelets so each patient gets only the specific component they actually need

Theory, procedure, precaution & significance of:

  • ABO and Rh blood grouping — the two blood group systems that matter most for transfusion safety, tested before every single transfusion
  • Cross-matching — directly mixing donor and recipient blood in the lab to physically confirm compatibility before transfusion, catching mismatches that basic grouping alone might miss
  • Direct and indirect Coombs' tests — detect antibodies already attached to red cells (direct) or free-floating antibodies in serum (indirect); the indirect test is exactly what screens for the incompatibility behind haemolytic disease of the newborn below

Also know: transfusion-transmissible infections and their screening (every donation is tested to prevent transmitting HIV, hepatitis, etc.), post-transfusion reactions (what happens when the safety system fails), and haemolytic disease of the newborn — where an Rh-negative mother's antibodies attack her Rh-positive baby's red cells; a classic Group C essay covering etiology, pathogenesis, clinical features, lab diagnosis, and management.
