05 Public Health (6 marks · 10 hrs)

Public health is the science of protecting and improving the health of whole communities — as distinct from clinical medicine, which treats one patient at a time. The spectrum of health runs from positive health (optimal wellbeing) through minor illness to disability and death, and "health" itself is defined by the WHO as complete physical, mental, and social wellbeing — not merely the absence of disease.

  • The functions and goals of public health center on disease prevention, health promotion, and prolonging life at the population level rather than the individual level
  • Its scope spans sanitation, nutrition, disease control, maternal-child health, and health education — most of which are their own separate units later in this subject
  • Important public health practices in Nepal include the immunization programme, safe motherhood initiatives, and community health worker networks — the same names come up again in MCH and Epidemiology below
  • Public health education feeds directly into diagnosis by shaping what a health worker screens for in a given community, based on locally common diseases and risk factors

06 Basic Epidemiology (9 marks · 15 hrs)

Epidemiology is the study of how disease is distributed in a population and what causes it to spread — the terminology below is dense but extremely high-yield for Group A, since each term has one precise, testable meaning:

Key terminology: isolation, quarantine, vaccine, zoonosis, epidemic, endemic, pandemic, sporadic, vector, host, reservoir, agent, incubation period, window period, incidence, surveillance, prevalence.

Every infectious disease is explained by the epidemiological triad — an agent (the pathogen), a host (the infected person), and an environment that lets the two meet; break any one link and the disease can't spread, which is the whole logic behind prevention measures.

  • Epidemiological measurements — incidence counts new cases in a time period; prevalence counts all existing cases at one point in time. Mixing these two up is a classic exam trap.
  • Dynamics of transmission — direct (person-to-person), indirect (via vehicle, vector, or air), and vertical (mother-to-child) routes
  • Infection prevention and control — hand hygiene, isolation of infectious patients, sterilization, and vaccination all break one link of the triad above
  • Immunity splits into active (the body makes its own antibodies, e.g. after a vaccine — slower but long-lasting) and passive (ready-made antibodies given directly, e.g. antivenom — fast but temporary). Nepal's National Immunization Programme is built on active immunity, given on a fixed schedule from birth.
  • Screening is explained by the iceberg phenomenon — for every visible, diagnosed case of a disease, there are many more hidden sub-clinical or undiagnosed cases below the surface, which is exactly why population-level screening programmes exist

07 Nutrition (9 marks · 15 hrs)

Nutrition is the study of nutrients and how the body uses them; a balanced diet supplies the right proportion of all nutrient classes for a person's age, activity level, and health status.

For protein, carbohydrates, fats, vitamins, and minerals: know definition, function, daily requirement, and deficiency disorder for each — this is the same nutrient-by-nutrient framework as Biomolecules in Year 2 Biochemistry, just applied at the population/public-health level instead of the biochemical level.

  • Malnutrition covers both undernutrition (deficiency — stunting, wasting, micronutrient deficiency) and overnutrition (excess — obesity); causes range from poverty and food insecurity to poor dietary knowledge
  • Food security means reliable access to enough safe, nutritious food; food hygiene prevents contamination; food adulteration is the illegal addition of inferior/harmful substances to food; food fortification is the deliberate addition of nutrients (e.g. iodized salt) to prevent deficiency at a population scale
  • Preservation techniques — drying, salting, refrigeration, canning, and pasteurization all work by the same principle: stopping microbial growth that would otherwise spoil the food

08 Maternal and Child Health (MCH) (11 marks · 20 hrs)

Safe motherhood covers a woman's care through pregnancy, delivery, and after — ANC (antenatal care) monitors the mother and baby before birth, catching complications early; PNC (postnatal care) does the same in the weeks after delivery, when many preventable maternal deaths actually occur. Obstetric referral is the system that moves a mother to higher-level care when a complication exceeds what the current facility can safely handle — a practical necessity in Nepal's mountainous terrain. Reproductive health is the broader concept these all sit inside: physical, mental, and social wellbeing in all matters relating to the reproductive system, not just pregnancy itself.

Breastfeeding

  • Technique matters for both milk supply and preventing nipple damage; breast milk storage guidelines exist so working mothers can express and safely store milk
  • Advantages of breastfeeding vs disadvantages of bottle feeding — breast milk provides antibodies and perfectly-matched nutrition at zero cost, while formula feeding carries contamination risk (especially with unsafe water) and no immune protection — this comparison is a recurring exam favorite
  • Common problems (engorgement, mastitis, poor latch) usually have straightforward fixes, but left unaddressed they're the most common reason mothers stop breastfeeding early

Immunization protects children during their most vulnerable early years — Nepal's current schedule delivers a fixed sequence of vaccines from birth through early childhood, building on the active-immunity concept from Unit 2. Family planning gives couples the objectives and methods (Nepal offers several, from short-acting to permanent) to control the timing and number of pregnancies — directly reducing maternal risk by spacing births safely.

09 Basic Hygiene and Sanitation (11 marks · 20 hrs)

All three pollution types below follow the same pattern — a source contaminates an environment that people then depend on, so prevention always means controlling the source rather than treating people after exposure:

Pollution typeCovers
Water pollutionContamination causes water-borne diseases (like cholera and typhoid from Year 2 Pathology); purification techniques (boiling, chlorination, filtration) break that transmission chain
Air pollutionFrom vehicle/industrial/indoor smoke sources; health effects range from respiratory disease to cardiovascular strain; preventive measures span both individual (masks) and policy-level (emission controls) action
Noise (sound) pollutionChronic exposure affects hearing and stress levels; prevention is mostly about source control and exposure limits

Also know: sterilization and disinfection techniques — the same concepts from Year 1 Microbiology, now applied at the community/household level instead of the lab bench.

10 Health Education and Health Promotion (4 marks · 7 hrs)

Health education is the deliberate process of giving people the knowledge and skills to make informed health decisions — it only works if the underlying communication process (a clear message, an appropriate method/medium, and a receptive audience) actually succeeds. Health education methods and media range from one-on-one counseling to mass media campaigns, chosen based on the audience and message. Its importance is straightforward: most of the disease prevention covered elsewhere in this subject (immunization uptake, safe water practices, family planning) depends on people actually knowing why it matters, not just on the service existing.

11 Primary Health Care (5 marks · 8 hrs)

Primary health care is the first point of contact between people and the health system — Nepal's national health policy and strategy are built around it specifically because it's the most cost-effective way to reach the most people, especially outside major cities.

  • Its elements cover exactly the ground this whole subject has walked through: health education, nutrition, safe water/sanitation, MCH services, immunization, disease control, and essential drug availability
  • Core principles — equitable access, community participation, and inter-sectoral coordination (health working together with education, water, and agriculture sectors)
  • The health worker's role is often to be the actual point of delivery for all of the above at the community level — which is exactly the role this CMLT qualification is training you toward

12 Waste Disposal System (11 marks · 20 hrs)

Waste is first split into biodegradable (breaks down naturally) and non-biodegradable (doesn't), since that distinction determines which disposal method actually works. The 3R concept — reduce, reuse, recycle — is the guiding priority order: reducing waste generation in the first place beats reusing, which beats recycling, which beats disposal.

  • Waste disposal stages — collection, storage, transportation, ultimate disposal — mirror the same "every stage matters" logic as the lab workflow in Year 3 Lab Management; a failure at any stage undermines the whole system
  • Disposal methods (sanitary landfilling, dumping, composting, incineration, burial) each suit different waste types — composting works for biodegradable waste, incineration for infectious/hazardous waste, and uncontrolled dumping is exactly the practice all of the above are meant to replace
  • Excreta disposal techniques prevent fecal-oral disease transmission — the same transmission route behind most of the intestinal infections in Year 2 Parasitology and Pathology
  • Vector control targets the mosquitoes and rodents that breed in poorly managed waste, directly cutting transmission of vector-borne diseases like malaria and scrub typhus

Hospital waste management

Hospital waste carries extra risk because it can be directly infectious — the hazards, separation practices, and incineration requirements are all more strict than general municipal waste. Mercury management matters specifically because older medical equipment (thermometers, some devices) contained mercury, a persistent environmental toxin if disposed of carelessly. The WHO hospital waste management guideline is the international reference standard Nepal's own protocols are built from, and it also covers the sources and safe management of liquid medical waste.

13 First Aid (14 marks · 25 hrs)

The highest-marks unit in this subject, and arguably the most immediately usable content in your whole first year — this is real, applied skill rather than pure memorization.

A first aider gives immediate, temporary care to an injured or suddenly ill person before professional help arrives — the goal is to preserve life, prevent the condition from worsening, and promote recovery, in that priority order.

Key terminology: casualty/victim, first aider, refer, trauma, antiseptic, immobilization, cyanosis, hemorrhage, stroke.

Every emergency assessment starts with the same sequence — CAB (Circulation, Airway, Breathing). Check circulation and control any severe bleeding first, then clear the airway, then confirm breathing — this order matters because uncontrolled bleeding kills faster than an airway problem. This sequence is the backbone of CPR and is almost guaranteed to appear as a Group C question.

Medical emergencies — causes, symptoms & first aid management

For each emergency below, the exam wants the same three things: what causes it, how to recognize it, and what to actually do — in that order. A few worth knowing cold:

  • Shock — the body's circulation fails to deliver enough blood to organs; recognize by pale/cold skin, rapid weak pulse, and confusion; manage by laying the person flat, elevating legs, and keeping them warm while arranging urgent transfer
  • Hemorrhage (severe bleeding) — apply direct firm pressure to the wound first, elevate the limb if possible, and only use a tourniquet as a last resort for a life-threatening limb bleed
  • Burns — cool the burn with running water for at least 10-20 minutes; never apply ice, oil, or toothpaste (common myths, but they worsen tissue damage)
  • Fracture/dislocation — immobilize the limb in the position found; don't try to realign the bone yourself
  • Snake bite — keep the person still and calm (movement speeds venom spread), immobilize the bitten limb below heart level, and get to a hospital fast; don't cut, suck, or apply a tight tourniquet (all outdated and harmful advice)

The rest of the named emergencies follow the same cause → recognize → manage pattern:

  • Intoxication/poisoning (insecticides, rodenticides, drugs, alcohol)
  • Foreign bodies in ear, nose, throat, eyes
  • Road traffic accidents
  • Frostbite
  • Strokes — recognize with the classic face-arm-speech signs
  • Acute mountain sickness — relevant given Nepal's terrain
  • Rabid animal bite — wash the wound thoroughly with soap and water for 15 minutes, a step that alone meaningfully reduces rabies risk
  • Insect bite
