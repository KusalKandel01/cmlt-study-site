04 Where To Spend Your Time

Prioritize

  • Prioritize Immunology (14 marks) and Virology (12 marks) — together over a third of the subject.
  • Memorize the named biochemical tests (indole, citrate, urease, catalase, oxidase, coagulase, TSI) as a table — Group A gold.
  • Learn the common-bacteria essay format (morphology → culture → disease → diagnosis → sensitivity) once, then apply it to each named organism.

Deprioritize

  • Don't try to memorize every unit equally — Units 1–8 total only 12 marks combined across 8 separate topics.
  • Don't skip Hospital Acquired Infection or QC in Microbiology because they're 1–2 marks each — they're the easiest marks in the subject to pick up.

  • External structures — flagella, fimbriae, pili
  • Covering: structure, composition and function of cell wall, cytoplasmic membrane, capsule

07 Morphological Classification of Bacteria (1 marks · 2 hrs)

Bacteria are classified by shape: Cocci (spherical), Bacilli (rod-shaped), Spirillum (rigid spiral), Vibrio (comma-shaped), Spirochaetes (flexible spiral).

08 Arrangements of Bacteria (1 marks · 2 hrs)

Common arrangement patterns: singles, pairs, clusters (including tetrads and sarcinae), chains, palisades, and Chinese-letter patterns — these arrangement clues help identify genus under the microscope.

09 Bacterial Growth (1 marks · 2 hrs)

  • Bacterial generation time — the time taken for a population to double
  • Bacterial growth requirements — nutrients, temperature, pH, oxygen
  • Bacterial growth curve — lag, log (exponential), stationary, and death phases

10 Staining (1 marks · 4 hrs)

Stain types: simple stain (single dye), differential stain (distinguishes bacterial types), negative stain (stains the background, not the cell).

11 Normal Bacterial Flora of the Human Body (1 marks · 2 hrs)

Normal flora provide beneficial roles (competitive exclusion of pathogens, vitamin synthesis) but can also play harmful roles (opportunistic infection when the host is immunocompromised or flora reaches a normally sterile site). Different body sites carry characteristic resident microbiota (skin, gut, oral cavity, etc.).

12 Sample Collection for Diagnosis of Bacterial Infections (1 marks · 1 hrs)

Common specimen types collected for bacteriology: blood, CSF, urine, fluid, stool, sputum, pus, and catheter tips — each with its own collection precautions to avoid contamination.

13 Media Preparation (6 marks · 10 hrs)

Culture media — a nutrient preparation used to grow microorganisms in the lab.

  • Classification by consistency
    • Solid
    • Semisolid
    • Liquid — each with its own advantages/limitations
  • Classification by function
    • Simple/basal media and special media
    • Enriched media — added nutrients (e.g. blood agar)
    • Enrichment media — favors growth of a target organism in a mixed sample
    • Selective media — inhibits unwanted organisms
    • Indicator media — shows a visible reaction (e.g. color change) for a specific organism
    • Differential media — allows visual distinction between organism types on the same plate
    • Transport media — preserves specimens in transit
    • Anaerobic culture media — for oxygen-intolerant organisms
  • Bacterial culture techniques
    • Definition of inoculum and colony
    • Streak, stroke, stab, and pour-plate methods

14 Controlling Microbial Growth in the Environment (6 marks · 10 hrs)

Sterilization — complete destruction of all microbial life, including spores.

  • Physical methods
    • Sunlight
    • Heat — dry heat (red heat, flaming, hot air oven) vs moist heat
    • Filtration
    • Radiation
  • Chemical methods
    • Phenol and phenolics
    • Alcohols
    • Halogens
    • Oxidizing agents
    • Heavy metals
    • Aldehydes
    • Gaseous agents

Sterilization vs disinfection vs antisepsis — sterilization kills everything including spores; disinfection kills most pathogens on inanimate objects; antisepsis kills/inhibits microbes on living tissue. Know standard safety precautions and biological waste disposal.

15 Controlling Bacterial Growth in the Body (2 marks · 4 hrs)

Antibiotics are classified by mode of action:

  • Inhibition of cell wall synthesis (e.g. penicillins)
  • Inhibition of protein synthesis (e.g. aminoglycosides, tetracyclines)
  • Disruption of the cytoplasmic membrane (e.g. polymyxins)
  • Inhibition of metabolic pathways (e.g. sulfonamides)
  • Inhibition of nucleic acid synthesis (e.g. fluoroquinolones)

21 Virology (12 marks · 20 hrs)

Viruses aren't cells — they're genetic material (DNA or RNA) wrapped in a protein coat, unable to reproduce on their own. That single fact explains almost every term below: a virus must hijack a host cell's own machinery to make copies of itself, which is why viral infections are so much harder to treat than bacterial ones (antibiotics target bacterial cell structures that viruses simply don't have).

Key terminology: inclusion body (a visible clump of viral particles inside an infected cell, sometimes diagnostic on its own), bacteriophage (a virus that infects bacteria specifically), paired sera (comparing antibody levels from early vs. recovery-phase blood samples to confirm a recent infection), cytopathic effect (visible cell damage caused by viral replication, used to detect virus growth in culture), and viral transport medium (a special solution that keeps a collected sample's virus intact and infectious until it reaches the lab).

Viruses are classified by their genetic material (DNA vs RNA), shape, and presence/absence of an envelope. Replication follows a fixed sequence — attachment to a host cell, entry, using host machinery to copy viral genes and proteins, assembly of new viral particles, and release (often bursting/killing the host cell in the process).

HIV and viral hepatitis are the highest-yield essay topic in this unit — for each, know structure, mode of infection, pathogenicity, and laboratory diagnosis:

  • HIV specifically targets and destroys CD4 T-helper cells, which is why advanced HIV infection (AIDS) leaves the body unable to mount an effective immune response to almost anything else — this single mechanism explains most of HIV's clinical picture
  • Viral hepatitis (A through E) all attack liver cells, but differ sharply in how they spread: Hepatitis A/E spread via contaminated food/water (fecal-oral), while B/C/D spread via blood and body fluids — a distinction that's a favorite differentiate-between question

Basic laboratory procedures for viral diagnosis follow the same logic as bacterial diagnosis, adapted for something too small to see under a normal microscope:

  • Viral culture — growing the virus in living cells (since viruses can't grow on ordinary media) and watching for cytopathic effect
  • Serological tests — detecting antibodies the patient's own immune system made against the virus
  • Immunofluorescence — using a fluorescent-tagged antibody to directly light up viral antigens in a sample
  • Electron microscopy — the only microscopy powerful enough to actually see a virus particle directly

22 Immunology (14 marks · 24 hrs)

The single highest-marks unit in this entire subject. Immunity is the body's ability to resist infection, and the whole unit builds from one core distinction: innate immunity is the fast, non-specific defense you're born with (skin, mucus, inflammation, general-purpose white blood cells) — it reacts the same way to any threat. Acquired (adaptive) immunity is slower to activate but highly specific, and — critically — it remembers, which is the entire principle behind vaccination.

Core terminology: an antigen is anything the immune system can recognize as foreign; a hapten is a small molecule too small to trigger a response alone but that becomes antigenic once attached to a larger carrier protein; an epitope is the exact small region of an antigen an antibody actually binds to; an adjuvant is a substance added to a vaccine to boost the immune response to it; MHC (major histocompatibility complex) is how cells display fragments of proteins on their surface for immune cells to inspect; complement is a cascade of blood proteins that helps destroy pathogens once antibodies have marked them.

The adaptive immune system has two coordinated arms: humoral immunity (B-cells producing antibodies that circulate in blood/lymph and neutralize pathogens from the outside) and cell-mediated immunity (T-cells that directly attack infected or abnormal cells). The primary immune response (first exposure) is slow and weak; the secondary response (repeat exposure) is fast and strong because of immune memory — so a second vaccine dose (booster) works far better than the first.

Antigen-antibody reactions are classified by what you can physically observe happening in the lab:

  • Agglutination — antibodies cross-link whole cells/particles into visible clumps (the basis of blood typing)
  • Precipitation — antibodies cross-link soluble antigens into a visible insoluble complex
  • Neutralization — antibodies physically block a toxin or virus from binding its target, without visible clumping
  • Complement fixation — antibody binding triggers the complement cascade, historically used to diagnose infections indirectly
  • ELISA — an enzyme-linked reaction produces a measurable color change; the workhorse test behind most modern antibody/antigen detection, including most rapid diagnostic kits

Hypersensitivity reactions are cases where this whole system overreacts and damages the body's own tissue — the introductory concept behind allergies and some autoimmune conditions, and worth a one-line Group A definition even without the full four-type classification.

23 Hospital Acquired Infection (1 marks · 1 hrs)

A hospital-acquired (nosocomial) infection is one a patient develops during a hospital stay that wasn't present or incubating at admission — a quick, one-line definition worth knowing since it's a direct laboratory quality-control concern (a lab that doesn't sterilize properly can literally cause these infections).

24 Quality Control in Microbiology (1 marks · 2 hrs)

General QC principles applied specifically to the microbiology lab — reproducibility of staining, media performance testing, and control organism use.
