04 Where To Spend Your Time

Prioritize

  • Plasmodium (malaria) is almost guaranteed to appear — know its full life cycle and lab diagnosis in detail.
  • This subject only carries 40 total marks (half of most others) — it rewards being thorough rather than selective.

Deprioritize

  • Don't neglect the reagent-preparation list in Laboratory Techniques — it's an easy, concrete Group A source.

05 Introduction to Parasitology (3 marks · 5 hrs)

TermDefinition
Definitive hostHost in which the parasite reaches sexual maturity
Intermediate hostHost in which the parasite undergoes larval/asexual development
Ectoparasite / EndoparasiteLives outside vs inside the host body
Temporary / Permanent parasiteContacts host briefly vs lives its whole life cycle on/in the host
Obligatory / Facultative parasiteMust live parasitically vs can survive either free-living or parasitically

Host-parasite relationships: commensalism (one benefits, other unaffected), mutualism (both benefit), pathogenic (parasite harms host). Also know the distinction between infection and infestation, and the classification of medically important parasites by site — intestinal, urine, blood, and tissue.

06 Protozoa (11 marks · 20 hrs)

Protozoa are single-celled organisms grouped by how they move: Rhizopoda (amoebae, moving by extending pseudopods — includes pathogenic, non-pathogenic, and free-living types), Ciliates (covered in tiny hair-like cilia), Flagellates (whip-like flagella), Kinetoplastida (flagellates with a distinctive DNA-containing organelle), and Sporozoa (non-motile, spore-forming — includes the malaria parasite).

For each organism below, know: prevalence, geographical distribution, mode of infection, habitat, life cycle, pathogenesis, laboratory diagnosis, and prevention/control — the classic Group C essay format. One distinguishing fact per organism, to anchor the rest:

  • Entamoeba histolytica — causes amoebic dysentery; spreads via contaminated food/water (fecal-oral), diagnosed by finding cysts/trophozoites in stool
  • Giardia lamblia — the most common intestinal protozoan parasite worldwide, causing prolonged diarrhea and malabsorption
  • Trichomonas vaginalis — unusual among this list for spreading sexually rather than via the fecal-oral route
  • Leishmania (L. donovani) — transmitted by sandfly bite, causes kala-azar (visceral leishmaniasis), diagnosed by finding LD bodies (Unit 13 of Hematology)
  • Plasmodium (P. vivax, P. falciparum) — the malaria parasites, transmitted by Anopheles mosquito bite; P. falciparum causes the most severe, potentially fatal disease
  • Toxoplasma gondii — acquired from undercooked meat or cat feces; particularly dangerous if a pregnant woman is newly infected, since it can cross the placenta
  • Cryptosporidium parvum and Cyclospora cayetanensis — both waterborne, causing watery diarrhea especially dangerous in immunocompromised patients
  • Balantidium coli — the only ciliate on this list, and the largest protozoan parasite of humans, acquired from pigs

07 Helminthes (Worms) (17 marks · 30 hrs)

The highest-marks unit in this subject. Same essay format as protozoa (prevalence, distribution, mode of infection, habitat, life cycle, pathogenesis, lab diagnosis, prevention/control) applies to each named organism below.

Nematodes (roundworms)

Nematodes have a simple cylindrical body and are, as a group, the most common worm infections globally — most spread via contaminated soil or food. Know the general life-cycle pattern (egg → larva → adult, often with a soil or intermediate-host stage) for the named species covered in class/past papers.

Cestodes (tapeworms)

Cestodes are flat, segmented worms that live in the intestine attached by a scolex (head), absorbing nutrients directly through their body wall since they have no digestive system of their own.

  • Taenia solium (pork tapeworm) and Taenia saginata (beef tapeworm) — acquired from undercooked meat; T. solium is the more clinically serious of the two since its larvae can migrate to the brain (neurocysticercosis)
  • Echinococcus granulosus — causes hydatid disease, forming slow-growing fluid-filled cysts in organs like the liver, acquired from dog feces
  • Hymenolepis nana — the only tapeworm here that can complete its entire life cycle in a single human host, making it the most common tapeworm infection in practice

Trematodes (flukes — intestinal, hepatic, pulmonary, blood)

Flukes are flat, leaf-shaped worms that virtually all require a snail as an intermediate host — a fact worth knowing since it explains why fluke infections cluster around specific water sources.

  • Fasciola hepatica — the liver fluke, acquired by eating contaminated aquatic vegetation (like watercress)
  • Paragonimus westermani — the lung fluke, acquired by eating undercooked freshwater crab or crayfish
  • Schistosoma haematobium — the blood fluke, acquired by skin penetration during contact with contaminated freshwater, notably causing urinary tract disease

08 Laboratory Techniques (9 marks · 15 hrs)

Every organism above is ultimately confirmed the same practical way: collecting the right specimen and looking for it directly under a microscope. Specimen types include stool, urine, blood, sputum, exudates, swabs, and aspirates — which specimen depends entirely on where that parasite lives in the body.

Reagents to know how to prepare: iodine solution (stains cysts for visibility), brine/33% zinc sulphate solution (both used for floatation concentration, since parasite eggs are less dense than these solutions and float to the surface), normal saline (for wet-mount preparation), Stoll's reagent (egg counting), Giemsa and Leishman's stain (blood parasite staining, shared with Hematology Unit 12), and benzidine solution (occult blood testing).

Laboratory techniques

  • Faecal (stool) examination — physical (color, consistency), chemical (reducing substances, occult blood), and microscopic (direct look for eggs/cysts) — the primary technique for most helminths and intestinal protozoa above
  • Blood examination by wet and stained smear, for blood parasites like Plasmodium and microfilaria — the same thick/thin smear technique from Hematology Unit 12
  • Urine and sputum examination for urinary parasites (like Schistosoma haematobium eggs) and respiratory parasites (like Paragonimus eggs)
  • Concentration methods (floatation and sedimentation) — since parasite eggs/cysts are often too sparse to spot in a plain smear, concentrating them first dramatically improves detection; floatation exploits low egg density, sedimentation exploits high egg density, so the right method depends on which parasite you're looking for

09 Practice Questions — Medical Parasitology

Group A · 1 mark each
  1. Define definitive host.
  2. Name two nematode species covered in this course.
  3. What reagent is used to detect malaria parasites on a stained smear?
  4. Define commensalism.
  5. Name the concentration method that uses zinc sulphate.
Group B · 3–5 marks
  1. Differentiate between definitive and intermediate hosts, with one example each.
  2. Describe the mode of infection and laboratory diagnosis of Entamoeba histolytica.
  3. Explain the floatation and sedimentation concentration methods for detecting intestinal parasites.
Group C · 8–10 marks
  1. Describe the life cycle, pathogenesis, and laboratory diagnosis of Plasmodium vivax and P. falciparum.
  2. Describe the mode of infection, life cycle, and laboratory diagnosis of Taenia solium.
  3. Discuss the prevalence, life cycle, and laboratory diagnosis of Fasciola hepatica.
