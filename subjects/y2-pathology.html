05 General Pathology (5 marks · 10 hrs)

Pathology is the study of disease — specifically, what disease actually does to cells and tissue, as distinct from anatomy (normal structure) or physiology (normal function). It splits into fields like clinical pathology (lab testing on patient samples, the focus of most of this subject) and anatomical pathology (examining tissue itself, covered in Year 3 Histopathology).

  • Pathology matters because it's the bridge between a patient's symptoms and an actual diagnosis — without it, medicine is just guessing from the outside
  • Diagnostic techniques used in pathology range from the gross/microscopic examination you'll do in Year 3 to the biochemical and hematological tests covered in your other Year 2 subjects — this whole subject is really the theory that ties all your lab subjects together

06 Cell Injury and Cellular Adaptation (20 marks · 35 hrs)

The highest-marks unit in this subject — a quarter of the total. Every disease process ultimately comes down to cells either adapting to stress or being damaged by it, which is exactly what this unit's terminology describes:

TermMeaning
NecrosisPathological, uncontrolled cell death from injury — always harmful, triggers inflammation
ApoptosisProgrammed, controlled cell death — the body's normal, tidy way of removing cells, no inflammation triggered
Hypoxia / IschemiaReduced oxygen supply / reduced blood supply to tissue — ischemia is usually the actual cause, hypoxia the resulting effect
AtrophyDecrease in cell/organ size — often from disuse or reduced blood/nerve supply
HypertrophyIncrease in cell size (not number) — e.g. a muscle enlarging with exercise
HyperplasiaIncrease in cell number — e.g. the uterus growing during pregnancy
MetaplasiaReversible change of one differentiated cell type to another — a protective adaptation to chronic irritation, but a warning sign since it can progress further
DysplasiaAbnormal, disordered cell growth — the actual pre-cancerous step that metaplasia can progress to
AplasiaComplete failure of an organ/tissue to develop at all

Notice the progression implied above: normal → adaptation (atrophy/hypertrophy/hyperplasia/metaplasia) → dysplasia → cancer — this is exactly why metaplasia and dysplasia are considered warning stages, not just vocabulary.

Mechanism of cell injury (biochemical basis)

Almost every injury pathway funnels down to the same four biochemical failures: decreased ATP production (the cell runs out of energy to maintain itself), mitochondrial damage (the ATP-producing machinery itself is destroyed), calcium influx disrupting normal cell signaling, and accumulation of oxygen-derived free radicals that directly damage cell membranes and DNA.

Whether injury is reversible or irreversible depends on how much of this damage has accumulated before the cause is removed — this distinction is exactly what separates a cell that recovers from one that undergoes necrosis. Also know: transplant rejection (the immune system attacking a transplanted organ as foreign) and autoimmune diseases (the immune system mistakenly attacking the body's own tissue) — both are, at their core, the immune system misapplying the same recognition mechanisms from Year 2 Immunology.

07 Inflammation (9 marks · 15 hrs)

Inflammation is the body's protective response to injury or infection — the terminology describes what you can actually observe: oedema (swelling from fluid leaking into tissue), erythema (redness from increased blood flow), vasodilation (the widened blood vessels causing that redness), abscess (a walled-off pocket of pus), and the fluid itself is classified as exudate (protein-rich, from inflammation) vs transudate (protein-poor, from simple fluid imbalance) — a distinction directly relevant to the fluid examination in Unit 6.

Acute inflammation is fast-onset and short-lived (the classic hot/red/swollen/painful response); chronic inflammation develops slowly and persists, often producing a granuloma (a walled-off cluster of immune cells) when the body can't fully clear the trigger — tuberculosis is the classic granuloma-forming example. Chemical mediators (like histamine) are what actually drive the vasodilation and fluid leakage described above.

08 Infection (17 marks · 30 hrs)

Key terminology here describes the full life of an infection in one person: the agent (pathogen) enters a host, possibly via a vector; the incubation period is the silent time between exposure and symptoms, while the window period specifically refers to the time before a test can detect the infection even though it's already present (a distinction that matters directly for HIV/hepatitis testing below).

For each disease below, know: etiological agent, route of transmission, pathogenesis, clinical manifestation, laboratory diagnosis, and treatment — in the Nepalese context specifically, since prevalence and presentation both matter locally:

  • Candidiasis — a fungal infection, often opportunistic in immunocompromised patients
  • Leprosy — caused by Mycobacterium leprae, historically significant in Nepal's public health history
  • Enteric fever (typhoid) — caused by Salmonella Typhi, transmitted fecal-orally like the intestinal parasites in Year 2 Parasitology
  • Cholera — caused by Vibrio cholerae, notorious for causing severe dehydration through massive watery diarrhea
  • Scrub typhus — transmitted by mite bite, an important cause of undiagnosed fever in Nepal
  • HIV/AIDS — the same virus covered in Year 2 Microbiology's Virology and Immunology units, now from a disease-pathology angle
  • Infectious hepatitis — covers the same hepatitis viruses from Microbiology, focused here on the liver pathology they cause
  • Japanese encephalitis — a mosquito-borne viral brain infection, a recurring seasonal concern in Nepal's Terai region

09 Neoplasia (9 marks · 15 hrs)

Neoplasia means new, uncontrolled cell growth — the direct continuation of the dysplasia progression from Unit 2. Key terminology: benign tumors stay localized and don't invade or spread; malignant tumors invade surrounding tissue and can undergo metastasis (spreading to distant sites via blood or lymph) — this single property (spread vs no spread) is the entire basis of the classic differentiate-between question. Carcinoma (from epithelial tissue), sarcoma (from connective tissue), and lymphoma (from lymphatic tissue) name malignant tumors by their tissue of origin, while papilloma and adenoma name benign ones. Anaplasia and pleomorphism both describe how abnormal and varied cancer cells look under the microscope compared to normal cells — the more anaplastic, generally the more aggressive the cancer.

Also know: the etiology of cancer (carcinogenic agents — chemical, physical/radiation, and viral), tumor markers (the same concept from Year 2 Biochemistry, now applied diagnostically), and laboratory diagnosis of cancer (biopsy and cytology, previewing Year 3 Histopathology & Cytopathology).

10 Specific Specimen Examination for Laboratory Diagnosis (20 marks · 35 hrs)

Tied with Unit 2 for the highest marks in the subject. For each fluid type, know indications, specimen collection, and physical/chemical/microscopic/microbiological investigation — and remember the exudate vs transudate distinction from Unit 3, since that's exactly what these fluid examinations are testing for.

  • Examination of effusions (synovial, pleural, pericardial, peritoneal fluids) — each is normal body fluid that's accumulated abnormally; distinguishing exudate from transudate tells you whether the cause is inflammatory/infectious or just fluid-balance related
  • Examination of urine — composition of normal urine, specimen collection, and investigations; directly builds on the urine formation mechanism from Year 1 Anatomy
  • Examination of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) — composition, collection (lumbar puncture), and how it changes during disease (e.g. cloudy/high protein in bacterial meningitis) — one of the highest-stakes specimens in this whole course since it directly reflects brain/spinal cord health

Semen examination

A structured, multi-parameter examination used primarily for fertility assessment:

  • Specimen collection requires specific abstinence-period instructions to standardize results
  • Volume, appearance, and liquefaction time — semen is normally gel-like at ejaculation and liquefies within a set time; delayed liquefaction can affect sperm motility
  • Fructose estimation and pH measurement — fructose (from the seminal vesicles) is a nutrient source for sperm; abnormal pH can indicate infection or obstruction
  • Percentage of motile/viable spermatozoa, sperm count, and percentage with normal morphology — together these three parameters are what actually determine fertility potential, each assessing a different aspect of sperm quality

11 Practice Questions — Clinical Pathology

Group A · 1 mark each
  1. Define necrosis.
  2. What is the difference between hypertrophy and hyperplasia?
  3. Define metastasis.
  4. Name two chemical mediators of inflammation.
  5. Define incubation period.
Group B · 3–5 marks
  1. Differentiate between benign and malignant tumors.
  2. Differentiate between leukemoid reaction... (see Hematology) — explain acute vs chronic inflammation instead, with causes and mechanism.
  3. Explain the mechanism of cell injury on a biochemical basis.
Group C · 8–10 marks
  1. Describe the etiological agent, transmission, pathogenesis, clinical features, and laboratory diagnosis of HIV/AIDS in the Nepalese context.
  2. Discuss the types of cell injury and the forms of cellular adaptation, with examples.
  3. Describe the physical, chemical, and microscopic examination of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and its diagnostic significance.
