08 Biomolecules (23 marks · 40 hrs)

The single biggest unit in the whole subject — nearly 30% of the marks. A biomolecule is any organic compound produced by a living organism; the four major classes below (carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, plus the supporting cast of enzymes, vitamins, minerals, and hormones) make up almost everything you'll estimate in a real clinical chemistry lab later in this course.

Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are the body's primary and fastest-access energy source, built from carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen in a roughly 1:2:1 ratio. They're classified by how many sugar units are joined together:

  • Monosaccharides — single sugar units that can't be broken down further (glucose, fructose, galactose)
  • Disaccharides — two monosaccharides joined by a glycosidic bond: sucrose (glucose+fructose, table sugar), maltose (glucose+glucose), lactose (glucose+galactose, milk sugar)
  • Oligosaccharides — a short chain of 3–10 monosaccharides
  • Polysaccharides — long chains; homopolysaccharides are built from one repeating sugar (starch, glycogen, cellulose — all pure glucose chains), heteropolysaccharides mix different sugar units

Proteins

Proteins are built from amino acids linked by peptide bonds, and unlike carbohydrates their job is rarely just energy storage — structure, enzymes, transport, and immune defense are all protein jobs (the same list you'd give for "functions of proteins" in Group C).

  • Amino acids are classified by whether the body can make them (non-essential) or must get them from diet (essential), and by their final metabolic fate
  • Proteins themselves are classified three ways: by function (structural, enzymatic, transport...), by chemical/solubility properties, and by nutritional value
  • Denaturation — a protein losing its functional 3D shape under heat, extreme pH, or heavy metals, without breaking its peptide bonds. This is why a fever above ~42°C is dangerous: body proteins start denaturing.
  • Coagulation (irreversible clumping, e.g. egg white cooking) and flocculation (reversible, loose clumping) are both visible results of denaturation

Lipids

Lipids are energy-dense, water-insoluble molecules used for long-term energy storage, cell membrane structure, and insulation.

  • Triglycerides (3 fatty acids + glycerol) — hydrolysis breaks them apart with water; saponification is the same reaction under alkaline conditions (the basis of soap-making); rancidity and lipid peroxidation are both forms of fat spoilage from oxidation
  • Saturated fatty acids have no C=C double bonds (solid at room temp, e.g. animal fat); unsaturated have one or more (liquid, e.g. plant oils). Essential fatty acids (like linoleic acid) can't be made by the body — deficiency shows up as skin lesions and poor growth.
  • Phospholipids form the cell membrane's bilayer; cholesterol is a structural lipid and the precursor for steroid hormones and bile acids

Plasma proteins, enzymes & vitamins

Plasma proteins (albumin, globulins, fibrinogen) each have a distinct normal range and a distinct electrophoretic pattern — meaning when you run a patient's serum on electrophoresis, disease states show up as a shifted band pattern, which is why this is a favorite Group C diagram question.

Enzymes are biological catalysts — proteins that speed up reactions without being consumed. Their activity depends on temperature, pH, substrate concentration, and enzyme concentration, and each enzyme has an active site shaped to fit one specific substrate (lock-and-key). Inhibition can be competitive (a molecule blocks the active site) or non-competitive (a molecule binds elsewhere and distorts the enzyme).

  • Coenzymes are small helper molecules (often vitamin-derived) an enzyme needs to function; isoenzymes are different molecular forms of the same enzyme found in different tissues — this is exactly why LDH, CPK, and ALP are diagnostically useful: which isoenzyme is elevated tells you which organ is damaged (e.g. cardiac vs liver vs bone)

Vitamins split into two solubility classes with very different behavior: fat-soluble (A, D, E, K) are stored in the body and can reach toxic levels; water-soluble (B-complex, C) aren't stored and need regular dietary intake. Each vitamin has a specific deficiency disease — this pairing (vitamin → deficiency disease) is a classic Group A/B pattern.

  • Minerals — the same biochemical-importance/deficiency framework from Year 1 Chemistry Unit 2.7, now applied specifically to Iron, Calcium, Phosphorus, Sodium, Potassium, and Iodine
  • Hormones — chemical messengers secreted directly into blood; classified as steroid, protein/peptide, or amine hormones by structure
  • Tumor markers — substances (often proteins) elevated in blood when certain cancers are present; used for screening, diagnosis support, and monitoring treatment response
  • Nucleic acids — built from a nucleoside (sugar + base) plus a phosphate group = nucleotide; DNA carries genetic information as a double strand using deoxyribose sugar, RNA is single-stranded and uses ribose

09 Metabolism of Biomolecules (20 marks · 35 hrs)

Carbohydrate metabolism

Once glucose enters a cell, its main breakdown pathway is glycolysis — a 10-step sequence that splits one glucose molecule into two pyruvate molecules, netting 2 ATP directly. It happens in the cytoplasm of every cell and doesn't need oxygen, which is exactly why it matters clinically: red blood cells (which have no mitochondria) rely on glycolysis alone for energy.

Gluconeogenesis runs the opposite direction — building new glucose from non-carbohydrate sources (amino acids, lactate, glycerol) — and matters most during fasting, when the body needs to keep blood glucose from dropping.

  • Cori's cycle — lactate produced by exercising muscle travels to the liver, gets converted back to glucose, and returns to the muscle: a metabolic recycling loop
  • Rapoport-Luebering cycle — a red-blood-cell-specific side branch of glycolysis that produces 2,3-BPG, which helps hemoglobin release oxygen to tissues
  • HMP shunt (pentose phosphate pathway) — an alternate glucose pathway that doesn't make ATP but generates NADPH (needed for fat synthesis and protecting cells from oxidative damage)
  • Glycogen metabolism — glycogenesis (building glucose into storage) and glycogenolysis (breaking it back down) are how the liver buffers blood sugar between meals

Blood glucose is kept in a narrow range hormonally: insulin lowers it (pushes glucose into cells, promotes storage), glucagon, cortisol, and epinephrine all raise it. When this system fails, you get Diabetes Mellitus — the single highest-yield essay topic in this unit:

  • Type 1 — autoimmune destruction of insulin-producing cells, usually childhood-onset, always needs insulin. Type 2 — insulin resistance, usually adult-onset, linked to obesity.
  • Cardinal symptoms — polyuria (excess urination), polydipsia (excess thirst), polyphagia (excess hunger), unexplained weight loss
  • Complications — retinopathy (eyes), nephropathy (kidneys), neuropathy (nerves), and cardiovascular disease — the same four are asked about repeatedly
  • Diagnosis hinges on the Glucose Tolerance Test (GTT) — patient fasts, drinks a measured glucose load, and blood glucose is measured at set intervals; a slow return to baseline confirms impaired glucose tolerance

Protein metabolism

The body maintains an amino acid pool — a shared supply drawn from diet and protein breakdown, used for building new proteins or, when in excess, converted to energy. Two reactions control this: transamination shifts an amino group from one amino acid to a keto-acid (no nitrogen is lost, just moved), while deamination removes the amino group entirely, producing ammonia — which is toxic and must be disposed of immediately.

That disposal happens via the urea cycle, which converts toxic ammonia into non-toxic urea in the liver for excretion by the kidneys. This is exactly why blood urea (BUN) is used clinically as a marker: high BUN can mean impaired kidney excretion, high protein breakdown, or a urea-cycle problem — you need to know which because they're clinically very different.

  • Creatine/creatinine — creatinine is produced at a near-constant rate from muscle creatine breakdown, making it a more reliable kidney-function marker than urea
  • Uric acid — the end product of purine (DNA/RNA base) breakdown; elevated levels cause gout
  • Bilirubin — the breakdown product of hemoglobin; elevated levels cause jaundice (covered in more depth under Liver Function Tests below)

Lipid metabolism

Stored fat is mobilized through β-oxidation — fatty acid chains are broken down two carbons at a time inside the mitochondria, each cycle producing a molecule of acetyl-CoA that feeds into the same energy-producing pathway used by carbohydrates. This is a highly efficient energy source (weight-for-weight, fat yields roughly double the ATP of carbohydrate).

When fat breakdown outpaces the body's ability to process the resulting acetyl-CoA — classically during fasting, starvation, or uncontrolled diabetes — the liver converts the excess into ketone bodies (ketogenesis). Peripheral tissues can burn these for fuel (ketolysis), but if they build up faster than they're used, blood pH drops and you get ketoacidosis, a genuine medical emergency and a well-known diabetes complication.

Fatty liver results when fat delivery/synthesis in the liver outpaces its ability to export or burn it — commonly linked to alcohol use, obesity, and diabetes, and a named health risk in the curriculum worth a one-line Group A definition.

10 Estimation of Biochemical Analytes (17 marks · 30 hrs)

This unit is where Unit 4's biomolecules turn into things you actually measure. For each analyte below, you need the method, principle, procedure, reference range, clinical significance, and precautions — but the marks-per-minute return is highest if you focus on clinical significance (what a high or low result means) since that's what Group B/C actually rewards over rote procedural steps.

Organ function tests — introduction & interpretation

Individual analytes above are usually ordered together as a panel targeting one organ system — this is the exam-favorite framing, since a Group C question is more likely to ask "what does an LFT panel tell you" than to ask about ALT in isolation.

  • Liver Function Tests (LFT) — bilirubin, ALT/AST, ALP, total protein/albumin together
  • Renal Function Tests (RFT) — urea, creatinine, electrolytes together
  • Pancreatic Function Tests (PFT) — amylase (and lipase) together
  • Cardiac Function Tests (CFT) — troponin and cardiac enzymes together, for suspected heart attack
  • Thyroid Function Tests (TFT) — TSH, T3, T4 together, for thyroid over/under-activity

12 Practice Questions — Clinical Biochemistry

Group A · 1 mark each

  2. Name the two types of amino acid deamination.
  3. What is the normal function of ALP?
  4. Define ketogenesis.
  5. Name any two fat-soluble vitamins.

  1. Explain the classification of enzymes and the factors affecting enzyme activity.
  2. Describe the pathway and clinical significance of glycolysis.
  3. Differentiate between isoenzymes and coenzymes with examples.
Group C · 8–10 marks
  1. Describe the types, cardinal symptoms, complications, laboratory diagnosis, and management of Diabetes Mellitus.
  2. Explain the classification, functions, and deficiency manifestations of vitamins.
