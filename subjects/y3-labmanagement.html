04 Where To Spend Your Time

Prioritize

  • Total Quality Management (15 marks) is the single biggest unit — memorize the QA terminology (accuracy, precision, SD, CV, sensitivity, specificity) cold.
  • Lab Hazards/Safety and Inventory Management are tied at 9 marks each — both are concrete, list-based, and fast to memorize.
  • Bio-safety and bio-security (BSL 1–4, risk groups) is a clean, self-contained topic — high marks-per-minute-studied.

Deprioritize

  • Don't neglect Medical Ethics thinking it's soft/non-technical — the accrediting-body list (NPHL, ISO, NABL, CAP, NHPC) is pure Group A recall.

05 Introduction to Clinical Laboratory Management (9 marks · 10 hrs)

This unit sets up the "why" behind everything else in this subject: a diagnostic lab isn't just a room full of instruments — it's a regulated part of Nepal's national healthcare delivery system, with its own management framework, code of conduct, and formal complexity classification.

  • Laboratory management framework — the organized structure of planning, staffing, quality, and safety that keeps a lab running reliably day to day, not just when things go right
  • Nepal's national healthcare delivery system places laboratories at every tier (from small health posts to central referral hospitals), so knowing where a lab sits in that system tells you what tests and complexity it's expected to handle
  • The code of conduct of the medical laboratory profession covers accuracy, confidentiality, and professionalism — this is the ethical foundation Unit 15 (Medical Ethics) builds on directly
  • Labs are classified by complexity level according to national guidelines — a basic health-post lab isn't expected to run the same tests as a tertiary hospital lab, and this classification determines staffing and equipment requirements

06 Departments/Sections of a Clinical Laboratory (5 marks · 6 hrs)

A real diagnostic lab isn't one room — it's organized into sections, each mirroring a subject you've already studied elsewhere in this course. Knowing the sections and what each one does is really just a map of your own curriculum:

  • Patient communication — where results actually reach the patient, the final step of the whole workflow
  • Sample collection — the entry point; errors here (wrong tube, wrong labeling) contaminate every result downstream
  • Haematology, Blood banking, Clinical biochemistry, Microbiology, Serology/immunology, Parasitology, Histopathology & cytopathology — each corresponds directly to a Year 2/3 subject you've already studied
  • Emergency lab — a fast-turnaround section for tests that can't wait (e.g. cardiac markers, blood gases)
  • Molecular laboratory — the newest section, running PCR and other DNA/RNA-based tests

07 Laboratory Design (5 marks · 6 hrs)

A lab's physical layout isn't arbitrary — every design choice below exists to either protect sample integrity, protect staff safety, or both.

  • Location and space requirement — enough room to separate clean and contaminated workflows, preventing cross-contamination
  • Light, ventilation, temperature control, humidity — many reagents and samples are sensitive to heat/humidity; poor ventilation also lets airborne biohazards accumulate
  • Electrical and water supply — reliable power is a patient-safety issue when an analyzer mid-run loses power
  • Sanitation and noise control, and a fume hood/safety hood for handling volatile or hazardous chemicals safely
  • Human resource planning at different levels — a lab is designed around its staffing model as much as its equipment

08 Laboratory Work Flow (5 marks · 6 hrs)

Every single test that happens in this course — from a urine dipstick to a PCR — follows the same four-stage work flow: appropriate specimen collection → specimen organization and transport → laboratory investigation → report preparation and patient communication. A break at any stage (wrong specimen, delayed transport, an unreported result) invalidates everything that came before it — so this simple four-step sequence is worth memorizing as a diagram, not just a sentence.

09 Personnel Management (4 marks · 4 hrs)

Staff duty rotation spreads workload and specialist coverage evenly across shifts, while continued medical education (CME) keeps lab staff current as tests, technology, and guidelines change — a lab is only as reliable as the people running it, so this short unit is really about the human side of the quality system built out in Unit 13.

10 Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) (5 marks · 5 hrs)

An SOP is a written, step-by-step instruction for performing a specific lab task the exact same way every single time, regardless of who's doing it — the entire point is removing variation between staff and between days.

  • Essential components: purpose, scope, required materials, the exact procedure steps, safety precautions, and references
  • Significance — SOPs are what make a lab's results reproducible and legally defensible; "we always do it this specific way" is the whole basis of lab accreditation

11 Clinical Laboratory Inventory Management (9 marks · 10 hrs)

This unit is the paperwork and record-keeping backbone that makes the workflow in Unit 4 actually auditable — every stage of testing leaves a paper (or digital) trail.

  • A Test Request Form (TRF) is the formal requisition that starts every test — without one, there's no legal record of why a sample was even collected
  • Lab report formats differ by section but always need to be traceable back to a specific patient, specimen, and date
  • Record keeping matters both for patient care continuity and for legal/audit purposes if a result is ever questioned later
  • Stock inventory and expiry charts — using an expired reagent silently produces wrong results, so tracking expiry isn't optional paperwork, it's a quality control step
  • Instrument maintenance sheets (refrigerator, incubator) and daily cleaning records prove equipment was actually working correctly on the day a result was produced
  • Logistics management keeps reagent/consumable supply chains from running dry mid-testing
  • A Laboratory Information System (LIS) automates most of the above in a modern lab — linking sample IDs, results, and reports without manual transcription errors

12 Lab Hazards and Lab Safety (9 marks · 10 hrs)

A diagnostic lab handles infectious samples, sharp objects, and reactive chemicals as a matter of routine — this unit is about the systems that keep that routine from becoming a routine injury. A working 0.5–1% hypochlorite solution and a ready spill kit aren't just supplies, they're the first line of response the moment something goes wrong.

  • Needle-stick injuries — wash immediately with soap and water, report per protocol, and start post-exposure assessment without delay; minutes matter for bloodborne pathogen risk
  • Spillage accidents — contain, disinfect, and dispose of contaminated material following a fixed protocol, not improvised on the spot
  • A Material Safety Data Sheet (MSDS) tells you exactly how to handle, store, and respond to an accident with a specific chemical — every reagent in the lab should have one accessible

Common lab hazards

Unsafe premises, naked flames/fire, microbial/biological hazards, chemical hazards and explosions, sharps and glassware, unreliable water supply, and pest infestation (ants, rodents) — each hazard category needs its own specific control measure, but they're all addressed by the same core toolkit: personal protective equipment (PPE), staff health monitoring, and disciplined equipment/procedural safety.

13 Total Quality Management (TQM) & Quality Assurance (15 marks · 15 hrs)

The single highest-marks unit in this subject — nearly a fifth of the total, and arguably the conceptual core of the whole course: how do you actually know a lab result is correct?

The TQM framework has four layers: Quality Assessment (checking outcomes after the fact), Quality Assurance (the overall system designed to prevent errors), Quality Laboratory Procedures (the specific documented methods), and Quality Control (the day-to-day checks that catch errors before they reach a patient) — split into internal QC (run by the lab itself, daily) and external QC (an outside body cross-checks the lab's performance periodically).

The Levey-Jennings (L-J) chart is how internal QC actually gets tracked day to day — control sample results are plotted over time against expected mean ± standard deviation limits, so a drifting or suddenly-erratic instrument becomes visually obvious before it produces a wrong patient result.

Terminology used in Quality Assurance

  • Accuracy — how close a result is to the true value; precision — how consistent repeated results are with each other, regardless of whether they're correct. A method can be precise but not accurate (consistently wrong).
  • Sensitivity — how well a test correctly identifies people who truly have the condition; specificity — how well it correctly identifies people who truly don't
  • A standard has a known, certified value; a control (positive, negative, high/normal/low sera) is run alongside patient samples specifically to verify the test is working correctly that day
  • Standard deviation (SD) measures spread around the mean; coefficient of variation (CV) expresses that spread as a percentage, making it comparable across different tests

When something does go wrong, sources of error are classified by where in the workflow they happened: pre-analytical (before testing — wrong sample, poor collection), analytical (during testing — instrument/reagent problems), or post-analytical (after testing — transcription or reporting errors) — most real lab errors are actually pre-analytical, which is why specimen collection (Unit 4) gets so much emphasis.

14 Bio-safety and Bio-security (9 marks · 10 hrs)

Bio-safety protects lab workers from the organisms they're handling; bio-security protects those organisms/samples from being accessed or misused by someone who shouldn't have them — a subtle but testable distinction.

  • Microorganisms are sorted into risk groups 1–4 by how dangerous and transmissible they are — risk group 1 (minimal individual/community risk) up to risk group 4 (high individual and community risk, e.g. highly transmissible agents with no available treatment)
  • Biosafety Levels (BSL 1–4) are the matching containment standards a lab must meet to safely work with each risk group — the higher the risk group of the organism, the more specialized the facility (airflow, PPE, access control) required

Laboratory waste management starts with segregating waste correctly, since mixing hazardous and non-hazardous waste turns everything into hazardous waste:

  • Colour-coded bins — red, blue, yellow, green, black, white, and dedicated sharps bins each correspond to a specific waste category (infectious, chemical, general, sharps, etc.), so segregation happens at the point of disposal, not after
  • Waste then follows proper treatment and disposal per category — the same 3R concept (reduce, reuse, recycle) from Year 2 Public Health applies here, now specifically to lab waste streams

16 Practice Questions — Clinical Laboratory Management

Group A · 1 mark each
  1. Define Standard Operating Procedure (SOP).
  2. What does BSL stand for?
  3. Define coefficient of variation.
  4. Name one colour-coded waste bin and what it's for.
  5. Define accuracy in a QC context.
Group B · 3–5 marks
  1. Differentiate between internal and external quality control.
  2. Explain the management of a needle-stick injury in the lab.
  3. Describe the components of laboratory work flow, from specimen collection to report.
Group C · 8–10 marks
  1. Describe the Total Quality Management framework and the terminologies used in Quality Assurance (accuracy, precision, sensitivity, specificity, SD, CV).
  2. Discuss laboratory hazards and the safety measures used to prevent them, including PPE and MSDS.
  3. Explain the risk group classification of microorganisms and the corresponding Biosafety Levels (BSL 1–4).
