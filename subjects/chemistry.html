05 Physical & General Chemistry (34 marks — All 14 Sub-units)

1.1 Introduction to Chemistry

Element — a pure substance of one atom type. Compound — a fixed-ratio chemical combination of elements. Radical — a group of atoms behaving as a single unit in reactions (e.g., SO₄²⁻). Balancing methods: hit-and-trial and partial equation method. Seven reaction types exist (combination, decomposition, displacement, double displacement, neutralization, oxidation, reduction).

1.2 Stoichiometry

  • Dalton's Atomic Theory — matter is made of indivisible atoms; atoms of the same element are identical
  • Mole concept — one mole = 6.022×10²³ particles (Avogadro's number); moles relate mass, volume, and particle count
  • Limiting reagent — the reactant that runs out first, determining how much product forms

1.3 Atomic Structure

  • Rutherford's model — atom has a dense positive nucleus with electrons orbiting around it (from the gold foil experiment)
  • Bohr's model — electrons orbit in fixed energy levels; explains the hydrogen spectrum
  • Aufbau principle — electrons fill lowest-energy orbitals first
  • Hund's rule — electrons fill empty orbitals singly before pairing up
  • Isotopes (same protons, different neutrons) vs isobars (same mass number, different elements)

1.4 Periodic Classification

Modern periodic law arranges elements by atomic number. Periodic trends (general pattern across a period/down a group): atomic radii, ionization potential, electron affinity, electronegativity — know which increase left-to-right vs top-to-bottom.

1.5 Chemical Bonding

  • Electrovalent (ionic) bond — electron transfer
  • Covalent bond — electron sharing
  • Co-ordinate covalent bond — both shared electrons come from one atom
  • Hydrogen bond — affects solubility, viscosity, and boiling point (explains why water has an unusually high boiling point)

1.6–1.8 States of Matter

StateKey laws/concepts
GaseousBoyle's Law (P∝1/V), Charles' Law (V∝T), ideal gas equation, Graham's law of diffusion
LiquidVapour pressure, surface tension, Raoult's law, osmosis and osmotic pressure (biological importance), colloids (Tyndall effect, Brownian movement, dialysis)
SolidAmorphous vs crystalline; efflorescent/deliquescent/hygroscopic solids

1.9 Oxidation & Reduction

Oxidation — loss of electrons (increase in oxidation number). Reduction — gain of electrons. Balancing by oxidation number method and ion-electron method.

1.10 Acid, Base, Salt

TheoryDefinition of acid
ArrheniusReleases H⁺ in water
Bronsted-LowryProton (H⁺) donor
LewisElectron-pair acceptor

Antacids — bases used medically to neutralize excess stomach acid — direct clinical relevance.

1.11 Electrochemistry

Strong vs weak electrolytes (degree of ionization). Arrhenius theory of ionization. pH and pOH — pH + pOH = 14. Buffer solution — resists pH change; explicitly named as important in the human body (blood buffering).

1.12 Volumetric Analysis

Titration — determining unknown concentration by reacting with a known solution. Normality, Molarity, Molality — different ways of expressing concentration. Indicator — shows the endpoint of a titration by color change.

1.13 Thermodynamics

Enthalpy change — heat absorbed/released in a reaction. Exothermic (releases heat) vs Endothermic (absorbs heat). Hess's Law — total enthalpy change is the same regardless of reaction pathway.

1.14 Chemical Kinetics

Rate of reaction — how fast reactants convert to products. Order of reaction (how rate depends on concentration) vs molecularity (number of colliding molecules). Catalysis — speeds up reactions without being consumed; enzyme catalysis is named specifically — the biological version, central to your future biochemistry coursework.

06 Inorganic & Environmental Chemistry (20 marks — All 9 Sub-units)

2.1 Hydrogen, Oxygen & Water

Ortho-hydrogen and para-hydrogen are the two spin isomers of the H₂ molecule, differing in the relative nuclear spin. Oxides are classified by how they react with water: acidic oxides (e.g., CO₂, SO₂) form acids, basic oxides (e.g., Na₂O, CaO) form bases, and amphoteric oxides (e.g., Al₂O₃, ZnO) can act as either.

Hard water contains dissolved Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ salts that stop soap from lathering; soft water doesn't. Two named softening methods:

  • Clark's process — adding calcium hydroxide (lime) to precipitate out the bicarbonates causing temporary hardness
  • Permutit (zeolite) process — passing hard water through sodium aluminium silicate, which exchanges its Na⁺ ions for the water's Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ ions

2.2 Nitrogen

Haber's process — the industrial method for manufacturing ammonia (NH₃) by reacting nitrogen and hydrogen under high pressure/temperature with an iron catalyst; the basis of most nitrogen fertilizer production. Ammonia acts as a Lewis base (electron-pair donor). Named identification test: ammonia gives a brown precipitate with Nessler's reagent — a classic qualitative test for ammonium ions, a common Group A question.

2.3 Carbon

Allotropes of carbon — the same element in different structural forms: diamond (hardest natural substance, tetrahedral structure), graphite (soft, layered, conducts electricity), and amorphous carbon. Carbon monoxide (CO) binds to hemoglobin roughly 200× more strongly than oxygen does, forming carboxyhemoglobin and blocking oxygen transport — the direct chemical mechanism behind CO poisoning.

2.4 Phosphorous

Phosphorous occurs biologically in bones and teeth (as calcium phosphate) and in the energy molecules ATP and ADP, where high-energy phosphate bonds store and release cellular energy.

2.5 Sulphur

Sulphur dioxide (SO₂) is a bleaching agent by reduction (temporary — can reverse on oxidation) and can act as either an oxidizing or reducing agent depending on the reaction.

2.6 Halogens

Chlorine (Cl₂), Bromine (Br₂), Iodine (I₂) — strong oxidizing agents with bleaching properties (permanent, unlike SO₂). Chlorine is the most reactive of the three and is widely used for water disinfection.

2.7 Metals & Minerals — Biological Importance

2.9 Pollution

Named pollutants: CO₂ (greenhouse gas), SO₂ (acid rain), O₃ (protective in the stratosphere, harmful at ground level), CO (toxic, binds hemoglobin), lead (neurotoxin), CFCs (ozone-depleting).

  • Greenhouse effect — trapped heat from CO₂/methane raising global temperature
  • Ozone depletion — CFCs breaking down stratospheric ozone, increasing UV exposure
  • Acid rain — SO₂/NOₓ dissolving in atmospheric water to form acidic precipitation

07 Organic Chemistry (26 marks — All 12 Sub-units)

3.1 Introduction to Organic Compounds

A functional group is a specific group of atoms within a molecule responsible for its characteristic reactions (e.g., -OH for alcohols, -COOH for carboxylic acids). IUPAC nomenclature is the systematic naming system based on the longest carbon chain and substituent positions. Clinically relevant drug classes to know by name and function:

  • Antipyretics — reduce fever (e.g., paracetamol)
  • Antiseptics — applied to living tissue to prevent infection
  • Analgesics — relieve pain
  • Antibiotics — kill or inhibit bacteria

3.2 Hydrocarbons

Alkenes and alkynes are typically prepared by elimination reactions (e.g., dehydration of alcohols, dehydrohalogenation of alkyl halides). Markovnikov's rule governs addition to unsymmetrical alkenes: hydrogen adds to the carbon that already has more hydrogens, and the other group attaches to the more substituted carbon.

3.3 Alkyl Halides

Substitution (SN1/SN2) replaces the halide with another group; elimination removes the halide plus a hydrogen to form a double bond. Saytzeff's rule — when elimination can form more than one alkene, the more substituted (more stable) alkene is the major product. Chloroform's named identification test — the Iodoform test: a positive yellow precipitate confirms a methyl ketone or a specific alcohol structure.

3.4 Alcohol

Alcohols are classified primary, secondary, or tertiary based on how many carbon groups attach to the carbon bearing the -OH. Fermentation of sugars by yeast enzymes produces ethanol — the biological/industrial route to ethyl alcohol.

3.5 Aldehydes & Ketones

Both contain the carbonyl group (C=O); aldehydes have it at the end of the chain, ketones in the middle. Both react with 2,4-DNP to form an orange/yellow precipitate — a named test confirming a carbonyl group is present.

3.6 Carboxylic Acid

Prepared by oxidation of primary alcohols or aldehydes. Acidic due to the -COOH group donating a proton (H⁺) — stronger acids than alcohols because the resulting carboxylate ion is resonance-stabilized.

3.7 Ethers

Prepared industrially and historically important in medicine — diethyl ether was one of the first general anesthetics used in surgery.

3.8 Aromatic Compounds

Benzene's Kekulé structure — a six-carbon ring with alternating single/double bonds (a simplified model; the real structure is a resonance hybrid). Characteristic reactions include halogenation and nitration — electrophilic substitution rather than addition, because substitution preserves benzene's stable ring.

3.9 Phenol

An -OH group directly attached to a benzene ring, prepared industrially. Kolbe's reaction converts sodium phenoxide + CO₂ into salicylic acid — the precursor used to make aspirin.

3.10 Nitrobenzene

Prepared by nitrating benzene; reduction of nitrobenzene produces aniline — the direct link to the next sub-unit.

3.11 Aniline

A weak base — its -NH₂ group's lone pair is partly delocalized into the benzene ring, making it a weaker base than typical amines. Prepared by reduction of nitrobenzene.

3.12 Biomolecules — the single most clinically relevant sub-unit in Chemistry

BiomoleculeKey points
CarbohydratesPrimary energy source; simple sugars (monosaccharides) vs complex chains (polysaccharides, e.g. starch, glycogen)
ProteinsBuilt from amino acids joined by peptide bonds; function includes structure, enzymes, transport, immunity; can be denatured by heat/pH
LipidsFats and oils; energy storage, cell membrane structure, insulation
VitaminsOrganic micronutrients required in small amounts for normal metabolism; fat-soluble (A, D, E, K) vs water-soluble (B-complex, C)
